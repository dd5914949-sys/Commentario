Geometry GM Tower: Futuristic Demo — Three Towers, One Vision

September 29, 2021 Andres Silva

The General Motors Renaissance Center in Detroit has always been a compelling compositional idea: multiple towers in deliberate relationship, each reinforcing the presence of the others. Taking that compositional logic as a starting point — three structures surrounding one — this project reimagines the arrangement in a thoroughly futuristic and fluid-like atmosphere.

What the Design Does

The renders reveal something that renders of single towers rarely capture: the behavior of light between structures. When the tower is compared to its digital geometric counterpart, the glimmer and refraction between the towers becomes one of the project’s most compelling qualities. Light enters the spaces between them at different angles throughout the day, creating dynamic shadow patterns and diffracted light that reaches floors that would otherwise require artificial lighting during normal working hours.

This isn’t incidental to the design — it’s a performance objective. Refraction and diffraction of natural light between towers reduces electricity demand on-site during peak working hours. What appears as a formal composition decision has measurable energy implications.

The Code’s Elegance

The Dynamo code that generates this entire building is, in the most literal sense, lightweight. Code snippets that appear minimal produce the complete tower complex — all of it exportable correctly to Revit for further development. The code is extraordinarily versatile: changing a handful of values changes the entire composition.

The final render also reveals the telescoping downward effect of the middle tower: the structure gets progressively more slender as it descends, creating a visual tension that makes the central tower appear to be pulled toward the ground by the forces it resists. Structural logic made visible — the same principle that drives all the best computational design work in this portfolio.

A Note on Rendering

This project initially encountered RAM limitations during rendering — dense geometry and complex material interactions pushed available memory to its limits. The operating system patch that introduced live RAM caching for render processes resolved this constraint, allowing the scene to complete. The patience required was considerable; the outcome justified it.
