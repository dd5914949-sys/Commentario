Part IV: Tower Project — Tessellated Voronoi Facade Pattern

July 20, 2021 Andres Silva

There’s a reason Voronoi patterns appear everywhere in nature — in the structure of bone, the venation of leaves, the cracking of mud. The pattern is nature’s solution to efficient space-division, and when applied to architectural facades, it produces something genuinely extraordinary: an organic geometric web that is structurally rational and visually captivating simultaneously.

From Tessellation Theory to Adaptive Components

Following the massing and site studies of Parts I through III, Part IV tackles the facade itself. The objective: apply a Voronoi tessellation pattern to the building’s faces using Dynamo’s adaptive component system — creating a skin where each panel conforms precisely to its position on the curved geometry.

This wasn’t straightforward. Voronoi generation on complex curved surfaces requires careful management of how seed points are distributed, how cells are bounded, and how adaptive component instances are placed and oriented relative to each face. Extensive research — blogs, YouTube, Dynamo library archives — eventually yielded the component logic needed to create this organic-looking web of panels.

The Solar Intelligence Embedded in the Pattern

Perhaps the most significant insight to emerge from this phase: the tessellated pattern isn’t just aesthetic — it’s the foundation for solar-responsive performance. To conduct a solar study on these panels, three points must be defined per instance, enabling the creation of normal vectors. By computing the dot product between each panel’s normal vector and the sun’s direction vector at different times of day, the script identifies which panels receive the most direct solar exposure.

This vector-to-parameter relationship enables something remarkable: by driving panel parameters from this solar data, the facade can automatically determine the best orientation strategy to reduce glare and unwanted solar radiation. Louvers open or close, panels rotate, creases develop or flatten — all driven by the mathematical relationship between surface orientation and solar angle.

The Technical Solution

The key breakthrough was adding a third reference point to each tessellated panel instance — allowing the adaptive component to establish a proper plane for normal vector calculation. With this in place, the solar study populated successfully across the entire facade surface.

The render required enormous patience — the geometry is dense, the materials complex, and memory limits were repeatedly tested. The patience paid off: the final visualization captures both the intricate web pattern and the subtle variation in panel orientation across the building’s faces, hinting at the kinetic potential embedded in the design.
