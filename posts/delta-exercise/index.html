Delta Exercise: Spiraling Geometry, Invisible Cores, and Self-Intersection

December 13, 2021 Andres Silva

Some exercises begin with a specific goal and end somewhere completely unexpected. This is one of those.

The starting point: geometry parametrization around a spinning axis. A column spiraling around an invisible central core while simultaneously moving further from it. Simple enough as a starting premise — the result is anything but.

From One Column to Many

The process began with a single column, encoding the rotation angle and watching how the geometry looked against the invisible core. Then another column was added, and another, until multiple columns were spiraling around the same invisible center in coordinated relationships.

The emerging form — at an intermediate stage — produced what felt like a yin-yang structure: balanced, harmonious, complete. But that wasn’t the target. The target was something more modern, more intricate, capable of self-intersection.

So we continued.

The Third Iteration: Telescoping Core

The third iteration introduced a telescoping middle core — a central element that unified the spiraling columns while adding visual focus and structural interest. The relationship between the rotating outer columns and the centralizing core created a new kind of tension in the form.

The challenge that makes this work genuinely engaging: sending geometry commands in sequences that “should not” work in that particular order, then discovering that they do — and produce geometry that couldn’t have been predicted from the individual rules that generated it. This freedom produces geometry that is unique, without obvious precedent in existing design libraries.

The Final Form: Self-Intersecting Multi-Core

The fourth and final iteration combined complex snippets from previous stages to produce a single entity of multi-core self-intersecting geometry. Follow any column from base to top and you will pass through two other core columns along the way.

This isn’t just visually dramatic — it has spatial implications for a built version of this structure. Air, resources, people, and ideas would flow between the intersecting volumes. Cross-pollination between departments occupying different sections of the building becomes structural — literally embedded in the architecture. Creating elevators for such a structure would be complex, but not impossible.

The structure, in its final form, resembles steel cable strands without their main core — tense, dynamic, impossible to look at from just one direction.
