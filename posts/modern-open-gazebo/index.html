Modern Open Gazebo: Reverse-Engineered Geometry, 90% Shade, 3D-Printed Recycled Legs

December 6, 2021 Andres Silva

This project began as a deceptively simple question: can a gazebo structure that looks organic and free-form be generated from pure mathematical logic? The answer, reached through extensive reverse engineering, several false starts, and deep dives into Dynamo documentation, is yes — and the result achieves something conventional construction rarely does.

The Structure’s Environmental Logic

The substructure is comprised of slated wooden planks that follow the surface of the entire volume, creating what appears from a distance as an illusional sliced layer diaphragm. This isn’t purely aesthetic: the layered effect creates a shaded region approaching 90% density in the most concentrated regions of the structure, dramatically lowering temperature beneath the canopy.

Critically, the shading is achieved without blocking natural ventilation or suppressing natural light entirely. Air flow is maintained through the gaps between slats; diffuse daylight filters through the layered surface. The result is a microclimate — significantly cooler than the surrounding exposed area — that doesn’t feel dark or enclosed.

The Legs: Finally Correct

The legs presented the most sustained challenge of the project. Previous iterations had placeholder geometry that failed to respond correctly to the forces the structure actually experiences. This update resolves that: the legs were modeled correctly and then optimized to reduce mass while increasing their response to applied forces.

The design direction: legs 3D-printed from recycled plastics, produced entirely on-site. This production method has profound implications — it eliminates transportation of structural elements (only raw feedstock material needs to move), reduces fabrication waste, and repurposes plastic material that would otherwise require disposal.

The Challenge That Made It Worth Doing

The reverse engineering challenge for this project was one of the most demanding encountered to date. The initial approach for tackling the inner shell geometry didn’t survive contact with the actual geometric constraints. An entirely new approach had to be derived from scratch — requiring new Dynamo nodes, new tutorials, and new thinking about how to extract structurally valid geometry from a complex free-form surface.

“This exercise pushed me out of my comfort zone, and it was great to learn how to utilize these new tools.”

That’s what good projects do.
