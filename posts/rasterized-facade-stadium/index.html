Rasterized Facade Stadium: A Football-Inspired BIM Landmark in Brussels

September 5, 2022 Andres Silva

Every architect has projects that consume them. This stadium is one of those — months of development, four major technical problems, and a design that tested the limits of what can be done with BIM, parametric coding, and determination.

The inspiration: football. My favorite sport. Brussels: a city that deserves a landmark.

Starting from Mass

The initial shape emerged from sculpting various layers about the Z-axis, creating the distinctive organic profile that distinguishes this stadium from the geometric regularity of conventional bowl designs. The form is not symmetrical by accident — it’s asymmetrical by algorithm.

Once the mass was defined, the structural logic could be introduced. Columns of the required thickness were placed radially based on the design algorithm’s requirements, following the beams that in turn follow the risers, creating a coherent load path from roof to ground.

The Voronoi Substructure

The panels on the stadium’s exterior don’t attach directly to the concrete structural frame — they’re offset from it, requiring a substructure to span the gap and hold them in position. For this, a Voronoi function creates the perfect solution: it divides the exterior surface into organic cells that provide attachment points while creating the irregular rasterized pattern visible across the facade.

The facade panels themselves were developed as custom parametric families that open in one direction only. Because of the underlying geometry of the building, these panels contort in varying directions as the facade curves — producing unexpected and dynamic opening patterns that wouldn’t have been possible with conventional panel layouts.

The Chair Rotation Problem — and Its Elegant Solution

The most technically satisfying problem of the project: after placing thousands of chair instances using a parametric line-subdivision approach, every single chair faced in the same direction — degree 0. All of them staring at the same side of the stadium.

Rotating them individually wasn’t feasible. Rotating them all simultaneously rotated them identically — which just moved the problem rather than solving it.

The solution arrived later: a short Dynamo script that gathers all chair instances and rotates each one by the angle required to face toward the center of the stadium. Two unknowns — the initial angle and the final angle of the rotation — were the only variables that needed resolution. Once identified, a few lines of code corrected all thousands of chairs simultaneously.

The modular floor system demonstrates the facility’s multi-sport capability: the render with the tennis court layout shows how the stadium can host different sports and entertainment events through floor reconfiguration.

Sustainable Materials Throughout

All concrete in this project incorporates industrial waste to carbon-seal the aggregate within the structure. All panels use recycled fiber composites. This isn’t a decorative sustainability claim — it’s a design constraint that drove material specification from the earliest stages.
