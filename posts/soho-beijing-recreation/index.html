SOHO Beijing Recreation: Reverse-Engineering a Structural Masterpiece

October 6, 2021 Andres Silva

Galaxy SOHO in Beijing is widely considered one of the most technically sophisticated building envelopes of the 21st century. Two glass skins, continuously rotating floor plates, and a structural system of extraordinary complexity. When you look at it, you understand the awards. When you try to model it, you understand the ambition it took to build it.

We modeled it anyway — because reverse-engineering great buildings is one of the fastest ways to develop genuine technical mastery.

The Double-Skin Strategy

The building’s defining characteristic is its dual glass facade — two independently mounted glass skins for radically different purposes. The outer skin manages solar radiation and weather; the inner skin provides thermal isolation and glare control at the interior envelope. Between them runs the building’s structural expression, visible through the glazing as an undulating, dynamic pattern.

The Structural Roadmap

The structural logic works through a load-transfer hierarchy: two tower spines running vertically and following the rotating floor geometry; ribbed underpinning connecting each floor plate to the spines; external columns that the inner ribs attach to. This arrangement distributes loads from core to skin to column, preventing the inner core from carrying the entire floor load — the result is a more efficient structure with dramatically reduced column sizes.

The Oscillating Floors

One of the building’s most extraordinary visual characteristics is floor-area oscillation: each floor plate grows and contracts as it rises, creating the impression of breathing. In the recreation, these area variations are generated parametrically — each floor defined by a rotation value and an area envelope, with the facade pattern emerging as a direct geometrical consequence.

The S-Shape Is Not Designed — It Emerges

The S-shaped pattern on the outer glass is not a design element. It is the geometrical consequence of floor rotation. As each floor plate rotates incrementally relative to the one below, the facade traces a sinusoidal curve. Accumulate this across dozens of floors, and the pattern wraps the entire tower — not drawn, not applied, but inevitable.

This is one of computational thinking’s most compelling demonstrations: forms that look intentionally complex are often the natural consequence of simple, elegant rules applied consistently. The mathematics was always there. The parametric model reveals it.
