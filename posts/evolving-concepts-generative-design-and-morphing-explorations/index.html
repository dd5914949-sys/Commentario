Evolving Concepts: Generative Design and Morphing Explorations

October 8, 2023 Andres Silva

Generative design has a reputation problem: people think of it as producing wild, organic forms that look impressive but can’t be built. That misunderstands what the technology actually does. At its best, generative design is an optimization engine — it explores far more possibilities than any human designer can manually test, and selects outcomes based on quantitative criteria that matter.

This study demonstrates what that actually looks like in practice.

The Problem Being Solved

The project evolves from the massing work in Part I, shifting focus to a mass additive generative design that manipulates rectangular volumes along three axes. The constraint is clear: maximize Built-Up Area while limiting the decrease in height to no more than eight floors from the highest peak to the second lowest level.

This constraint reflects a real design objective — creating a terraced tower profile that reads as a coherent composition while maintaining meaningful floor area at every level.

Unraveling the Code

The code that drives this study is elegantly simple: it creates points along X and Y axes, treats each volume individually with its own height, width, and depth parameters, and then unions the resulting solids. The union operation is critical — it unlocks feedback on the combined form’s floor area, surface area, and volume, providing the quantitative data the generative solver needs to evaluate each configuration.

The solver explores the parameter space systematically, populating generations of solutions and selecting progressively better configurations based on the objective function. Grey filters in the solver output indicate the selected value — 85 meters on the B1-Height column, corresponding to 25 floors at 3.4 meters per floor — aligning precisely with the projections from Part I.

What the Morphed Form Will Be

The optimal configuration identified through generative design promises a tower that echoes the characteristics of the earlier massing study in three specific ways: height, terraced geometry, and optimization precision. A morphing diagram will further refine the facade features in the concluding stages of this process.

The satisfaction in this work comes from the integration: using multiple generative tools on a single project, letting quantitative optimization and qualitative judgment operate together. The tools explore; the designer selects; and the result is better than either could produce alone.
