Before the Algorithm: How Gaudí and Calatrava Invented Parametric Design Without a Computer

Two Spanish masters derived form from performance constraints — 100 years apart. What their methods mean for computational architecture today.

June 19, 2026 Verdi Studio

Two Spanish masters derived form from performance constraints — 100 years apart.

Introduction

There is a common misconception about parametric design: that it is a product of software.

Open Grasshopper and the assumption follows naturally — sliders, data trees, algorithmic geometry. The tools feel new. The visual language feels of this decade. And so the idea takes hold that performance-driven, computationally generated form is a recent invention, born in the 1990s when McNeel released Rhino and practitioners began wiring together digital logic the way Gaudí’s contemporaries laid stone.

It is a comforting narrative for software companies. It is not accurate.

Two architects — Antoni Gaudí, working in Barcelona between the 1880s and 1926, and Santiago Calatrava, working across Europe and the Americas from the 1980s to the present — independently arrived at the same fundamental insight: that structural performance, when followed with enough discipline, generates form. Not the other way around.

They did this without computers. One did it with hanging chains and mirrors. The other did it by studying skeletons.

Part One: Antoni Gaudí and the Physical Solver

Antoni Gaudí was born in 1852 in Reus, Catalonia. He studied architecture in Barcelona, graduating in 1878 — at which point the director of the school reportedly remarked: “We have given this academic title either to a madman or a genius. Time will tell.”

The genius became evident quickly. What was less obvious was the rigour behind the apparent madness.

Gaudí was obsessed with a single structural problem: how do you build forms of extraordinary complexity without the buttresses and flying supports that Gothic architecture required to prevent collapse? Buttresses are structural admissions of failure. They exist because the primary form could not carry its own load. Gaudí wanted forms that needed no apology — geometry that was inherently stable.

His solution came from physics.

The Hanging Chain Model

For the design of the Colònia Güell Chapel (begun 1898), Gaudí developed what is now regarded as one of the most elegant structural design methods in architectural history: the hanging chain funicular model. The principle is this: a chain suspended between two points falls into a natural catenary curve — the shape that pure tension produces. When that curve is inverted, you get the corresponding arch in pure compression, with no bending forces to resist. It is, structurally speaking, the most efficient arch you can make.

Gaudí suspended a network of weighted chains from a frame, each weight representing a structural load — walls, vaults, piers. The entire network shifted and settled into its catenary configuration under gravity. Above a mirror below the frame, Gaudí could look at the reflected image and see the building — arches, columns, vaults — rendered upside-down by physics.

He was running a physical simulation. The chains were the algorithm. Gravity was the solver. The resulting geometry was the optimised output.

This is not poetry or metaphor. A finite element analysis — the kind that runs inside Karamba 3D or SAP2000 today — solves for the same structural equilibrium state. The mathematics is identical. Gaudí’s chains solved it with gravity instead of matrix algebra.

The Sagrada Família, which Gaudí began in earnest in 1883, applies the same logic at monumental scale. Its branching columns, hyperboloid vaults, and helicoid surfaces are not ornamental choices. They are the geometric consequence of structural load paths traced to their logical conclusion. Nature does the same thing in tree branches and bone structure.

Part Two: Santiago Calatrava and the Anatomy of Form

Santiago Calatrava was born in 1951 in Valencia — the same eastern Mediterranean coast of Spain where Gaudí’s Catalan tradition had flourished seventy years earlier. He studied architecture at the Polytechnic University of Valencia, then moved to Zurich to take a second degree in civil engineering at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology (ETH). This dual education — architect and structural engineer — was not incidental. It was the foundation of everything that followed.

Before Calatrava produces a design, he draws. Not buildings — bodies. Vertebrae. Ribcages. The mechanical structure of a human jaw. The way load distributes across the femur. The articulation of a bird’s wing under aerodynamic stress.

His bookshelves, documented in multiple interviews and monographs, hold anatomical drawings alongside engineering texts. This is not affectation. Biology, Calatrava has argued repeatedly, has been solving structural optimisation problems for hundreds of millions of years under extreme selection pressure. The resulting forms — curved, branched, tapered, articulated — are exactly the forms that performance logic produces when followed honestly.

The Milwaukee Art Museum — A Wing That Opens

Nowhere is this more literal than the Burke Brise Soleil of the Milwaukee Art Museum (2001). The movable sun-shade structure — a set of steel fins spanning 66 metres, opening and closing like a bird’s wings twice daily — was derived directly from Calatrava’s study of avian anatomy. The mechanism is not a stylistic gesture. It is a functional solar shading device whose form was generated by asking: what is the most structurally efficient way to open, shade, and close a large spanning structure? Nature answered that question with wings.

The Turning Torso — Form From a Structural Problem

The Turning Torso in Malmö, Sweden (completed 2005) makes Calatrava’s argument at the scale of a 190-metre residential tower.

The building began explicitly as a sculptural study of a twisting human torso. But translating that form into a structural reality demanded a solution to a genuine engineering problem: how do you build a tower that rotates without tearing itself apart at the twists? Conventional column-and-slab construction cannot accommodate the torsional forces this geometry generates.

Calatrava’s answer: a structural spine of nine pentagonal segments, each independently rigid, collectively rotated 90 degrees from base to apex. An external steel exoskeleton runs the full height of the tower on its southern face, carrying the lateral and torsional forces that the rotation generates. The spine does what a vertebral column does — it provides axial rigidity while allowing the segments above and below to rotate in relation to each other.

The form and the structure are inseparable. Remove the exoskeleton and the building fails. Remove the rotation and the exoskeleton is unnecessary. The architecture is the engineering, expressed.

This is the same principle Gaudí enacted with chains a century earlier: let performance constraints generate form, then trust what they produce.

Part Three: The Shared DNA — What Connects Them

Calatrava has been explicit about Gaudí’s influence. In interviews he describes his predecessor as extending a tradition in Spanish architecture that treats structure not as a technical problem to be solved before architecture begins, but as the primary medium of architectural expression itself. Published research comparing their work notes that both architects favour “structural systems where the shape is determined by funicular polygons, which lend the building a vague resemblance to the skeleton of a mythical animal.” The language is vivid but the observation is structurally precise.

Both share five characteristics that define their shared lineage:

1. Performance before form. Neither architect begins with an aesthetic target and engineers toward it. The form is a consequence of performance logic pursued without compromise. This is genuinely rare in architectural practice. Most buildings are designed in the other direction.

2. Nature as structural library. Gaudí’s catenary curves appear throughout the natural world — in spiderwebs, fabric drape, any flexible material under gravity. Calatrava’s anatomical references — ribs, femurs, spinal columns — are biological solutions to the same optimisation problems he faces in construction. Both treat nature not as an aesthetic source but as a structural database.

3. Interdisciplinary formation. Gaudí studied craft and art alongside engineering. Calatrava holds degrees in both architecture and civil engineering, and also produces sculpture and painting. Neither was purely technician or purely artist. Their work exists in the precise space between.

4. Spanish Mediterranean roots. Both emerge from a specifically Iberian tradition more comfortable with ornamental complexity and structural daring than the austere rationalism of Northern European modernism. The Catalan and Valencian regional contexts — rich material cultures, deep craft traditions — shaped an attitude toward form that is foreign to the Miesian grid.

5. Long, iterative process. Gaudí spent 43 years on the Sagrada Família. Calatrava is famously iterative — he redraws and rethinks through physical models and sketches before committing to direction. Both understood that the best structural logic takes time to resolve.

Part Four: What This Means for Parametric Practice Today

The tools Gaudí and Calatrava used were entirely physical. Chains, weights, mirrors, anatomical drawings, clay models, hand calculations. The insight — that form should emerge from performance — required no software to arrive at.

What software has changed is the speed of iteration and the range of performance inputs that can be incorporated simultaneously.

Grasshopper is — in functional terms — a digital hanging chain model. You define parameters (loads, geometry, material properties), connect them through logic, and the system resolves toward a solution. Instead of gravity settling chains, algorithms settle geometry. The principle is unchanged. The iteration speed is orders of magnitude faster.

Ladybug adds a dimension that neither Gaudí nor Calatrava had: climate performance. Solar radiation, wind exposure, daylight hours, annual energy loads can now function as generative inputs in the same way structural load was a generative input for the hanging chain. A facade can be optimised for solar gain in winter and shading in summer not by applying a fixed design solution, but by letting the analysis drive the geometry.

Karamba 3D brings structural analysis back into the loop — the digital equivalent of Calatrava’s anatomical reasoning, with real-time feedback on stress, deflection, and material efficiency.

Together, these tools make it possible to practise architecture the way Gaudí and Calatrava always practised it: starting from performance, deriving form, trusting what the logic produces. The aesthetic quality of the result — when the reasoning is rigorous — tends to take care of itself. Calatrava made this observation about anatomy: efficient structures tend to be beautiful ones. The same principle applies whether you’re working with chains, bones, or algorithms.

Where Verdi Studio Stands

This lineage is not decorative context. It is the foundation of why Verdi Studio exists.

The dominant model in architectural practice still treats sustainability analysis as a late-stage verification exercise — something you run after the form is resolved, to confirm or challenge what the architect has already decided. The analysis arrives at Stage 3. The form was locked at Stage 1. By the time the numbers come back, changing them would require rebuilding the project from scratch, and no client will pay for that.

Gaudí did not validate his chains after building. He used them to find out what to build. That sequence matters enormously.

At Verdi Studio, environmental analysis — solar, energy, daylight, structural — runs before the first massing decision is made. The parametric model evolves as the analysis evolves. Climate performance is a generative input, not a compliance checkbox.

This is, in the most direct sense, the same practice Gaudí was performing in 1898. The tools are faster. The performance inputs are richer. The principle is identical.

Form follows performance. When it does, the beauty tends to follow too.

The Takeaway

Parametric design did not begin with software. It began with a question: what does structural performance want to look like?

Gaudí answered with chains. Calatrava answered with anatomy. Both produced buildings of extraordinary formal complexity that required no external justification — because the justification was built into the geometry from the beginning.

For anyone working in computational design today, this lineage matters. The algorithm is not the architect. It is the hanging chain. The architect is the one who understands what the chain is trying to say.
